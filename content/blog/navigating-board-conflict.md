Conflict between a pastor and a church board is one of the most common, and most poorly handled, dynamics in church life. It's common because a board and a pastor are bound to see things differently sometimes — that's not dysfunction, it's the natural result of putting several thoughtful people with real authority in a room together. It's poorly handled because most pastors were never taught how to disagree productively with the people who also happen to supervise them.

We've walked alongside pastors through this exact tension more times than we can count, and the pattern holds up consistently: conflict handled early and directly strengthens a board, while conflict avoided quietly erodes it.

Left unaddressed, board conflict doesn't stay contained. It leaks into staff meetings, into the pastor's preaching, into the congregation's sense of stability. Learning to navigate it directly and well is one of the more important, and more learnable, skills in pastoral leadership.

## Separate the Person from the Position

The first move in any board conflict is internal: can you separate what a board member is saying from who they are to you? A board member who pushes back hard on a proposal is not automatically against you, even if it feels that way in the moment. Most conflict with a board comes from a genuine difference in perspective or information, not from personal animosity.

Pastors who default to feeling personally attacked when a board disagrees will either avoid necessary conflict altogether or respond defensively when it does surface. Neither serves the church. Learning to hear "I disagree with this decision" as information rather than as a verdict on your leadership changes the entire tone of how conflict gets handled.

## Get Curious Before You Get Defensive

When a disagreement surfaces, the instinct many leaders have is to explain, justify, or persuade. A more productive first move is to get curious. Ask the board member or the board as a whole: "Help me understand what's behind this concern." Often, what looks like resistance to your idea is actually a legitimate concern about something you hadn't considered — a financial risk, a relational dynamic with a longtime member, a past experience that shaped their caution.

Curiosity does two things at once. It surfaces information you may be missing, and it signals to the board that their input actually matters, which builds the trust you'll need for the next disagreement.

## Address Conflict in Private Before It Becomes Public

Board conflict handled well in private rarely needs to become a public matter. Board conflict avoided in private almost always eventually becomes public, usually in a worse form — a tense exchange in an open meeting, a rumor that spreads through the congregation, a faction that forms around one side or the other.

If you sense tension building with a specific board member, reach out directly and privately before the next meeting. A short, honest conversation — "I want to understand where you're at on this before we're all in the room together" — defuses far more than it costs.

## Use a Third Party When Trust Has Eroded

Some conflicts go beyond a single disagreement and into a pattern of mistrust between a pastor and one or more board members. When that happens, trying to resolve it alone often makes things worse, because both sides are now interpreting everything through a lens of suspicion. Bringing in a neutral outside voice — a denominational leader, a ministry coach, or a trusted consultant — gives both sides a structured, less charged way to work through what's actually happening.

Asking for outside help here isn't a sign of weak leadership. It's a sign of a leader who values the relationship and the church enough to not let pride keep the conflict unresolved.

## Know the Difference Between Disagreement and Opposition

Not all board conflict is the same, and it's important to diagnose which kind you're facing. A board member who disagrees with a specific decision but remains committed to you and to the church's mission is in a fundamentally different position than a board member who has become an ongoing source of opposition to your leadership itself. The first calls for dialogue and patience. The second may call for a harder, more structured conversation, possibly involving denominational oversight or governance processes, about whether that person can continue to serve on the board in a healthy way.

Confusing these two — treating every disagreement as an attack, or tolerating persistent opposition as if it were just healthy debate — leads pastors astray in both directions.

## Conflict Handled Well Builds Trust

Boards and pastors who navigate conflict directly, honestly, and with genuine curiosity about each other's perspective often come out of a disagreement with more trust than they had before, not less. The goal isn't to eliminate conflict from board life — that's neither possible nor healthy. The goal is to build the skills and the relational safety to work through it in a way that strengthens the church rather than quietly damaging it.
