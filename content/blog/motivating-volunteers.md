Most churches don't have a shortage of willing people. They have a shortage of well-placed people. A congregation can be full of members who genuinely want to serve and still struggle to fill the nursery schedule or find someone to lead worship planning. The problem is rarely willingness. It's usually placement.

At The Hardy Group, we've sat with enough ministry leaders to know that a struggling volunteer program is almost never a sign of an uncommitted congregation — it's a sign that discovery and placement haven't caught up with the size of the church. That's the lens we bring into every coaching conversation on this topic.

When someone serves out of their gifting, motivation takes care of itself. When someone serves out of obligation or guilt, you'll spend the rest of your relationship with them managing burnout, resentment, or slow disengagement. If you want a volunteer culture that lasts, the goal isn't to convince more people to say yes. It's to get the right people saying yes to the right things.

## Start with Discovery, Not Recruitment

Most volunteer recruitment starts backward. A need opens up — the parking team is short two people, the greeting ministry has a gap — and leaders start asking anyone who might say yes. That approach fills slots, but it rarely produces motivated volunteers.

A better starting point is discovery. Before you ever have an opening to fill, you need a way to know your people: what they're good at, what energizes them, what kind of work drains them. This can be as simple as a short gifts and interests conversation during a new members class, or as structured as a spiritual gifts assessment paired with a follow-up conversation. The tool matters less than the habit. Churches that consistently ask, "what has God built into you?" before asking "what do we need you to do?" end up with volunteers who stay.

## Match the Person to the Work, Not the Work to the Calendar

Once you know someone's giftings, resist the urge to plug them into whatever is open. A gifted administrator forced into a hospitality role because that's where the need was will burn out fast, even if they're a wonderful person who loves the church. The mismatch itself is exhausting, regardless of how good the cause is.

Instead, build a mental (or literal) inventory of your people's strengths, and let that inventory shape how you develop new ministry roles. Sometimes this means creating a role that didn't exist before because you noticed someone's gift didn't have anywhere to go. A member with an ear for administration might become the volunteer who manages your scheduling software, even if there wasn't a formal "volunteer scheduler" role until you built one around them.

## Give People Ownership, Not Just Tasks

Motivation drops sharply when volunteers are treated as pairs of hands rather than partners in ministry. A greeter who is simply told to "stand at the door and smile" will do that and nothing more. A greeter who understands the purpose behind the role — that they are often the first spiritual impression a visitor has of your church — and who has some say in how they carry it out, will bring creativity and initiative you didn't ask for.

Whenever possible, hand volunteers a piece of ownership: let them help shape the process, solve the problems they encounter, and make small decisions within their area. Ownership converts a task into a calling. People rarely burn out doing something they feel is genuinely theirs.

## Say Thank You in Specific, Not Generic, Terms

"Thanks for serving" is a nice sentiment that volunteers hear so often it stops registering. What actually motivates people is specific recognition tied to the impact of their gift of service. Instead of a general thank-you from the platform, tell the volunteer coordinator directly: "The way you reorganized the check-in process cut our Sunday morning chaos in half — that's made a real difference for first-time families." Or “A 29-year old and a 34-year old gave their lives over to Jesus this morning. Your service in the nursery made that possible. Thank you!” Specific gratitude tells a volunteer their particular gift mattered, not just that a task got done.

This kind of feedback loop does more for long-term motivation than any incentive program. People want to know their gift landed somewhere real.

## Watch for the Warning Signs of Misalignment

Even with good discovery and good placement, giftings and roles can drift out of alignment over time. A volunteer who was energized by children's ministry two years ago might be quietly exhausted by it now, either because life circumstances changed or because their gifts have grown in a different direction. Leaders who check in periodically — not just when there's a problem — catch this early and can redirect a volunteer before frustration turns into resignation.

A simple annual conversation, even five or ten minutes, asking "Are you still energized by this?" can save you from losing a good volunteer entirely. Most people don't quit serving because they stop loving the church. They quit because nobody noticed they were serving in the wrong seat.

## The Long View

Motivating volunteers to serve with their best giftings isn't a program you launch once. It's a leadership posture you maintain — paying attention to people, matching them thoughtfully to the work in front of you, giving them room to own it, and telling them specifically when and why it matters. Churches that do this well don't have to beg for volunteers. They have people who show up because the work fits who God made them to be, and that kind of motivation doesn't run out.
