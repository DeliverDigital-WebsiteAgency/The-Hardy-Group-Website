Talking about money from the pulpit makes many pastors uncomfortable, and that discomfort often shows. Sermons on giving can come across as apologetic, defensive, or purely transactional — framed around what the church needs rather than what generosity does in the life of the giver. The result is a congregation that gives out of guilt or obligation rather than conviction, and a pastor who dreads the topic every time it comes around.

We've coached pastors through entire stewardship seasons built around exactly this shift — moving generosity teaching out of the budget conversation and into genuine, ongoing discipleship.

Challenging people to grow in generosity is a legitimate and important part of discipleship. Scripture has far more to say about money than almost any other practical topic, precisely because how we handle it reveals so much about the state of our hearts. Done well, teaching on giving isn't a fundraising tactic. It's genuine pastoral care.

## Teach Giving as Discipleship, Not Budget Support

The most important shift is framing. When giving is talked about primarily in terms of the church's budget or building needs, people give — if they give at all — the way they'd pay any other bill: reluctantly and at the minimum required. When giving is taught as a spiritual discipline that shapes a person's heart, their trust in God, and their freedom from the grip of money, the motivation changes entirely.

This means teaching on generosity should show up regularly in the normal rhythm of discipleship teaching, not just during a fall stewardship series or when the budget is behind. If giving only comes up when the church needs money, people will always hear it that way, no matter how it's phrased.

## Tell Real Stories of Changed Hearts

Abstract teaching about generosity rarely moves people the way a real story does. When someone in your congregation has grown in generosity and it's changed something in their life — their anxiety about money, their marriage, their sense of purpose — their story, told with permission, does more to challenge others than another sermon point ever could.

Real stories also remove the sense that generosity teaching is really about the pastor or the institution wanting something. A member testifying to what giving has done in their own life reframes the entire conversation around personal transformation rather than institutional need.

## Be Transparent About Where Money Goes

People are far more willing to be challenged in their giving when they trust how the money is used. Regular, clear communication about the church's finances — not just an annual report buried in an email, but visible, understandable updates about what giving accomplishes — builds the trust that makes people receptive to a challenge to give more.

A congregation that has no idea where their tithes go will resist any appeal, no matter how biblically grounded, because trust is the foundation underneath generosity, not a separate issue from it.

## Challenge Specific Next Steps, Not Vague Generosity

"Give generously" is too vague to act on. People respond better to a specific, challenging next step they can actually take: increasing their giving by a percentage point, committing to their first tithe, giving toward a particular need they can see the impact of. A specific challenge gives people something concrete to pray about and respond to, rather than a general sentiment they can nod along with and forget by Tuesday.

This is especially effective when paired with a personal invitation to reflect — asking people to consider, prayerfully and honestly, what their current giving reflects about their trust in God, and what a next step of faith might look like for them specifically.

## Model It from Leadership

A pastor and church leadership team who are known, even in general terms, to be generous givers themselves carry far more credibility when they challenge the congregation. This doesn't require disclosing specific numbers, but leaders who can speak from personal conviction — "this is something my own family has wrestled with and grown in" — teach with an authenticity that a purely instructional sermon can't match.

## Address the Anxiety Directly

Much of the resistance to giving isn't about doctrine. It's about fear — fear of not having enough, fear of financial instability, fear that comes from past experiences of scarcity. Teaching on generosity that never addresses this underlying anxiety will bounce off people who are silently overwhelmed by their own financial pressure. Naming that fear directly, and connecting generosity to trust in God's provision rather than denial of real financial concerns, makes the challenge land as pastoral rather than tone-deaf.

## Generosity as a Fruit of Discipleship

Challenging people in their giving isn't about closing a budget gap. It's about inviting people into a fuller experience of trust, freedom, and worship through how they steward what they've been given. Pastors who teach this consistently, transparently, and with real stories of transformation find that their congregations don't just give more — they give more freely, and that freedom is the actual goal.
