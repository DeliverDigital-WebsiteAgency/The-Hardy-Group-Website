Perseverance is one of the great virtues of ministry, and rightly so. Scripture is full of leaders who kept going long after it would have been easier to stop. But perseverance and stubbornness can look identical from the inside, and one of the hardest skills a ministry leader ever develops is telling the difference — knowing when to keep pressing forward and when it's time to let something go.

This is a conversation we have often in personal coaching sessions with pastors, because from the outside it's usually easy to see when something has run its course. From the inside, in the middle of it, it rarely feels that clear.

Ministry leaders are often trained, implicitly if not explicitly, to see quitting as a spiritual failure. That instinct has kept a lot of good work alive through hard seasons. It has also kept a lot of dying programs, unhealthy staff relationships, and draining commitments on life support far longer than they should have been.

## Not Every Ending Is a Failure

The first shift needed is theological, not strategic. Scripture doesn't treat every ending as defeat. Seasons change. Ministries have life cycles. Paul left churches, handed off responsibilities, and moved on to new work more than once. Ending something well — with honesty, care for the people involved, and a clear sense of what God accomplished through it — is a legitimate form of faithfulness, not a lesser one.

Until a leader believes this at a gut level, they'll keep confusing "ending" with "failing," and that confusion will keep them holding onto things long past their useful life.

## Ask What the Original Purpose Actually Was

A useful diagnostic question for any ministry, program, or staff relationship that's struggling: what was this originally meant to accomplish, and is it still accomplishing that? A midweek program that was launched to reach young families twenty years ago, now attended mostly by the same six retirees, may still be valuable — but it isn't doing what it was created to do. That's not automatically a reason to end it, but it is a reason to ask honestly whether it's the best use of the resources it's consuming.

The trap many leaders fall into is evaluating a ministry by whether it's "still good," rather than whether it's still fulfilling its purpose relative to what else that time, money, and energy could accomplish.

## Watch for the Sunk Cost Trap

Ministry leaders are just as vulnerable to sunk cost thinking as anyone else. "We've already invested five years and thousands of dollars into this" feels like a reason to keep going, but it's actually a reason that has nothing to do with whether continuing is wise. The money and years are already spent regardless of what you decide next. The only real question is: given where things stand today, is continuing the best next step, or is it time to redirect that energy elsewhere?

Naming this trap out loud — with a board, a staff team, or in your own reflection — makes it much easier to separate the emotional pull of past investment from a clear-eyed decision about the future.

## Distinguish Discouragement from Discernment

Every difficult season in ministry brings the temptation to quit something. The challenge is that discouragement and genuine discernment can produce the exact same feeling: a strong pull to walk away. The difference is usually visible over time, not in the moment. Discouragement tends to lift once you've had rest, prayer and perspective. Discernment tends to persist and often gets confirmed by trusted people around you.

This is why decisions to cut your losses in ministry should rarely be made alone, and rarely in the lowest moment of a hard week. Bring in a board member, a mentor, or a coach who isn't as close to the situation and give the decision time to settle before you act on it.

## Cutting Losses Well Still Takes Care

Deciding to end something is only half the work. Ending it well — with honest communication, gratitude for what it accomplished, and care for anyone who will feel the loss — matters just as much as the decision itself. A ministry that's ended abruptly or dismissively leaves wounds that outlast the program itself. A ministry that's closed with clarity and appreciation leaves people able to grieve, understand, and move forward with trust in their leadership intact.

## Build a Regular Review Rhythm, Not Just a Crisis Response

Most decisions to cut losses in ministry happen reactively, in the middle of a painful season, when a program or relationship has already become a visible problem. A healthier pattern is to build a regular, low-stakes review rhythm into the life of the church — an annual look at every ongoing ministry, program, and commitment, asking honestly whether each one is still serving its purpose. This turns "should we end this" from an emotionally loaded emergency question into a normal part of stewarding the church well, and it tends to surface the need to cut losses earlier, when the cost of doing so is much lower.

## A Different Kind of Faithfulness

Cutting your losses in ministry isn't the opposite of faithfulness. Done with discernment, it's an expression of it — a willingness to steward limited time, energy, and resources toward what God is actually doing now, rather than what he was doing five years ago. Leaders who learn to recognize the difference between perseverance and stubbornness free themselves, and their people, to invest fully in the work that's actually bearing fruit.
