There's a particular irony in pastoral ministry that almost every pastor experiences at some point: the person whose job is to help others grow spiritually can find their own personal walk with God quietly starved by the demands of ministry work. Sermon preparation, counseling, meetings, and crisis care can all involve Scripture and prayer without ever amounting to genuine personal time with the Lord. Protecting that personal time takes deliberate effort, because ministry will fill every available hour if a pastor lets it.

This is one of the most personal conversations we have in one-on-one coaching with pastors, because it's also one of the easiest things for a pastor to quietly let go of without anyone noticing.

## Distinguish Ministry Work from Personal Devotion

The first and most important distinction a pastor needs to hold onto is that studying a passage to preach it is not the same as sitting with that passage personally before God. Both are valuable, but they serve different purposes, and it's easy for the professional task to quietly replace the personal one. A pastor can spend hours in Scripture every week for sermon preparation while their own soul goes unfed, because they never approached the text simply as someone seeking God, rather than someone preparing content for others.

Naming this distinction honestly is the first step toward protecting real personal time, since it's impossible to guard something you haven't clearly separated from your job description.

## Guard a Specific Time, Not Just an Intention

A vague intention to "spend time with God sometime during the day" almost always loses to whatever urgent task shows up first. Pastors who successfully protect this time tend to guard a specific, consistent slot — often early morning, before the demands of the day begin — treated with the same non-negotiable seriousness as a scheduled appointment. This doesn't have to be rigid or joyless, but it does need to be specific enough that it isn't simply the first thing sacrificed when the calendar gets full.

## Tell Someone and Ask for Accountability

Personal devotion is easy to quietly let slide because, unlike a missed sermon or a skipped meeting, no one else notices right away. Pastors who share this commitment with a spouse, a close friend, or a coach or mentor, and genuinely invite them to ask about it, create an external accountability that internal willpower alone often can't sustain during demanding seasons.

This kind of accountability works best when it's not framed as performance review, but as genuine care — someone who checks in not to judge, but because they know how easily this gets lost and want to help protect it.

## Separate the Practice from Results-Driven Pressure

Because pastors are professionally responsible for spiritual growth, there's a temptation to treat personal devotional time the same way — evaluating it by whether it produced a usable insight or sermon illustration. This subtly turns personal time with God into another form of professional productivity, and can leave a pastor feeling like time with the Lord "didn't accomplish anything" if it didn't yield something useful for ministry.

Protecting genuine personal time means being willing to sit with Scripture and prayer with no goal beyond the relationship itself — some days producing nothing that will ever show up in a sermon, and treating that as entirely fine.

## Build in Retreat, Not Just Daily Rhythm

Daily personal time matters, but it isn't sufficient on its own. Pastors need longer stretches — a half day, a full day, an occasional multi-day retreat — away from the ongoing demands of ministry, specifically to reconnect with God without the pressure of preparing anything for anyone else. Churches and boards that build this expectation into a pastor's role, rather than treating it as an indulgence to be squeezed in if time allows, protect something that benefits the whole congregation, not just the pastor.

## Recognize the Cost of Neglecting This

A pastor whose personal walk with God has quietly withered while their public ministry continues is one of the most common and most dangerous patterns in long-term ministry burnout and moral failure. Preaching, counseling, and leading from a well that isn't being personally replenished eventually runs dry, often in ways that surface publicly and painfully. Protecting personal time with the Lord isn't a luxury for a healthy pastor. It's a safeguard against some of ministry's most serious risks.

## Give the Board Permission to Ask

Boards that genuinely care about their pastor's long-term health should feel free to ask, directly and periodically, how their pastor's personal walk with God is actually doing — not as a performance check, but as real pastoral care for the person leading the church. A pastor who knows their board cares about this, not just their preaching output, is far more likely to protect it seriously themselves.

## Protecting the Source

A pastor's ministry flows out of their relationship with God, not the other way around. Protecting personal time with the Lord isn't one more item on an already full calendar. It's the thing that makes everything else on that calendar sustainable, and it deserves the same deliberate protection a pastor would insist on for any other essential, easily neglected priority in the life of the church.
